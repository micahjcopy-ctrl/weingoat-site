The turn

The night I stopped trusting my game.

I lost $5,000 in one session. At the time that was a serious piece of my bankroll. The night itself didn't hurt. The next morning did. For the first time I didn't trust my game, and I didn't play for over a month.

What got me back wasn't a new strategy. It was the trunk of the tree. I dropped down in stakes, played the fundamentals, A, B and C, and forgot about the rest of the alphabet. The way a shooter in a slump goes back to free throws. A month later I was back at my normal stakes.

Then came the week that didn't feel real in the other direction. A win on the drive down to Hollywood, Florida. A satellite seat into a bigger event. A final table at two in the morning. Second place the next day, and my biggest score that same week. About $25,000 in seven days.

I didn't play differently in those two stretches. Same decisions, opposite results. That's variance, and it gave me the only rule I still play by.

If I can honestly say I played my best, I don't care about the result. The only time I'm upset after a loss is when I misplayed a spot.Adam

So what does "my best" actually mean? It's one question, asked at every decision. Here's the question.

The river

How do I maximize this spot?

There's no solver line for the whole game. Losing players ask "what am I supposed to do here?" Winning players ask how to get the most out of the spot in front of them. I answer it in three moves.

1

Ready

Before I sit down: am I in the headspace to play my best? If the answer is no, the best decision of the session is not to play it. Most leaks start before the first hand.

2

Bucket the table

Within a few orbits I've put every player in a type. The fastest tell is how many hands they play. Profitable poker is tight; someone in every pot is there to splash around.

3

Adjust, every street

Fear, greed and ego. Every decision at the table is one of the three. Name which one your opponent is showing and the "tough" spots get trivial.

Three players you'll meet tonight, and the move against each.

The gambler

Plays every pot, chases every draw, barrels when it's cheap.

Bluff less, value bigger. Theory says a third of the pot with top pair. He's calling anyway, so bet the whole pot.

The nit

Waits for premium hands and only puts chips in with a monster.

Be the aggressor. Nobody makes a strong hand every time. Pressure him and he overfolds a huge part of his range.

The LAG

Loose and aggressive. Raises everything, every hand, and dares you to call.

Take the initiative back. You can't call down with nothing. Sometimes the answer is to double his aggression.

One rule I almost never break: I don't bluff into an uncapped range. If he can still have the nuts, my bluff isn't a bluff. It's a donation.
