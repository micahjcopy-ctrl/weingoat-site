The flop

Where the money actually leaks.

If you've played a year or two, you learned the game from charts and free videos. Both assume the player across from you plays like a solver. Nobody at your table does.

So the leaks aren't in your cards. They're in the spots the charts never covered, and they look the same at every table I've sat at. None of them are a talent problem. They're a training problem, and that's the good news, because training is fixable.

Each one is a few big blinds a session. Over a year, those blinds are the whole gap between the player you are and the one you already suspect you could be.

  • Calling the big bet with a marginal hand because "he could be bluffing."

    Bad players over-convey strength through their sizing. Theory says call. At your table it's a trivial fold, and you keep paying it off.

  • Bluffing into a range that can still have the nuts.

    It works once. You remember the once. The long run doesn't.

  • Bleeding out when you're card dead.

    Two hours without a playable hand, and you watch your stack shrink instead of 3-betting the open from the one player who isn't tight.

  • Running one strategy against every table.

    The same line against a gambler, a nit and a maniac gets three different results. Most players never adjust, so the results look like variance. They aren't.

I know every one of these because I had every one of them. Here's what happened when they closed.

The night I stopped trusting my game.

I lost $5,000 in one session, a serious piece of my bankroll at the time. The night didn't hurt. The next morning did. For the first time I didn't trust my game, and I didn't play for over a month.

What got me back wasn't a new strategy. It was the trunk of the tree: I dropped down in stakes, played the fundamentals, A, B and C, and forgot about the rest of the alphabet. The way a shooter in a slump goes back to free throws. A month later I was back at my normal stakes.

Then came the week that didn't feel real in the other direction: a win on the drive down to Hollywood, Florida, a satellite seat, a final table at two in the morning, second place the next day. About $25,000 in seven days, playing exactly the way I'd played the night I lost. That's variance, and it gave me the only rule I still play by.

If I can honestly say I played my best, I don't care about the result. The only time I'm upset after a loss is when I misplayed a spot.Adam

What closed the leaks wasn't a chart. It was one question, asked at every decision. Here's the question.

The river

How do I maximize this spot?

There's no solver line for the whole game. I stopped asking "what am I supposed to do here?" and started asking how to get the most out of the spot in front of me. The answer runs in three moves, and you can run all three at your next session.

1

Ready

Before the first hand: am I in the headspace to play my best? If the answer is no, the best decision of the session is not to play it. Most leaks start before the cards do.

The first session you skip because the answer was no is the first session you don't donate.

2

Bucket the table

In the first few orbits, every player goes in a type. The fastest tell is how many hands they play. Profitable poker is tight; someone in every pot is there to splash around.

A few sessions in, the buckets fill themselves and the table stops feeling random.

3

Adjust, every street

Fear, greed and ego. Every decision at the table is one of the three. Name the one across from you and the "tough" spot gets trivial.

The spots you used to dread become the spots you look for.

Three players you'll meet tonight, and the move against each.

The gambler

Plays every pot, chases every draw, barrels when it's cheap.

Bluff less, value bigger. Theory says a third of the pot with top pair. He's calling anyway, so bet the whole pot.

The nit

Waits for premium hands and only puts chips in with a monster.

Be the aggressor. Nobody makes a strong hand every time. Pressure him and he overfolds a huge part of his range.

The LAG

Loose and aggressive. Raises everything and dares you to call.

Take the initiative back. You can't call down with nothing. Sometimes the answer is to double his aggression.

One rule I almost never break: I don't bluff into an uncapped range. If he can still have the nuts, my bluff isn't a bluff. It's a donation.
